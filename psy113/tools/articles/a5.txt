based_on: Hunt, M. G., Marx, R., Lipson, C., & Young, J. (2018). No more FOMO: Limiting social media decreases loneliness and depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 37(10), 751–768. https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751

== Abstract
Many studies have linked heavy social media use with poorer mental health, but most have relied on correlational designs and self-estimated screen time.
This study tested experimentally whether limiting social media use would improve the well-being of university students.
After a baseline week, 143 undergraduates were randomly assigned either to limit Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 minutes per platform per day or to use social media as usual for three weeks.
Social media use was measured objectively using screenshots of each participant's phone battery usage screen.
Students who limited their use showed significantly larger decreases in loneliness and depression than students in the usual-use group, and the reduction in depression was strongest among students who started the study with higher levels of depression.
Both groups showed similar reductions in anxiety and fear of missing out, which may reflect the benefits of simply monitoring one's own use.
The findings suggest that reducing, rather than eliminating, social media use may benefit students' mental health.

== Introduction
Social media platforms such as Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat have become a central part of daily life for most university students, many of whom check their accounts dozens of times a day.
These platforms allow students to stay in touch with friends and family, share experiences and join communities with similar interests.
At the same time, concerns have grown that heavy use may harm young people's mental health, particularly during the transition to university, when many students feel isolated.
[cd] Loneliness refers to the distressing feeling that one's social relationships are fewer or less satisfying than one would like.
[cd2] Depression, in this study, refers to a persistent low mood accompanied by symptoms such as loss of interest, poor sleep, low energy and feelings of worthlessness.
[cd3] Fear of missing out (FOMO) refers to the worry that others are having rewarding experiences from which one is absent, together with a desire to stay continually connected with what others are doing.
Large survey studies have found that young adults who spend more time on social media tend to report more loneliness and depressive symptoms (Primack et al., 2017).
Similar associations have been reported between social media use and anxiety, poor sleep and lower self-esteem.
[gap] However, because these studies were correlational, they cannot tell us whether social media use leads to poorer mental health or whether people who are already lonely and depressed simply spend more time online.
[gap~] In addition, most earlier studies relied on participants' own estimates of their screen time, which research has shown to be inaccurate and often biased.
A small number of studies have asked participants to quit Facebook completely for a week, but quitting entirely is unrealistic for most students and the effects of a single week may not last.
[tf] One explanation for the link between social media and poorer well-being comes from social comparison theory (Festinger, 1954), which proposes that people evaluate their own lives and abilities by comparing themselves with others.
[tf~] On social media, users mostly see carefully selected, positive images of others' lives, which may lead to upward comparisons in which people feel that their own lives are less exciting or successful.
Displacement is a second possible mechanism, as time spent scrolling may replace face-to-face interaction, sleep or other activities that protect mental health.
[rq] The present study aimed to test experimentally whether limiting social media use, measured objectively, would improve students' well-being over three weeks.
[h1] It was hypothesised that students who limited their social media use would show a greater decrease in loneliness than students who continued to use social media as usual.
[h2] It was also hypothesised that students who limited their use would show a greater decrease in depression than students in the usual-use group.
[h3] Finally, because limiting use might reduce social comparison, it was predicted that the limited-use group would show greater reductions in anxiety and FOMO.

== Method
[method] The study used a randomised experimental design with two conditions and measurements taken before and after the three-week intervention.
[sample] Participants were 143 undergraduate students (108 women, 35 men) at a private university in the north-eastern United States, who owned an iPhone and had active Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat accounts.
Participants were recruited through the psychology department's research pool and received course credit for taking part.
During the first week, all participants used social media as normal so that baseline levels of use and well-being could be recorded.
[od] Social media use was measured objectively: each evening, participants sent the researchers screenshots of their iPhone battery usage screen, which shows the number of minutes spent in each app.
[manip] Participants were then randomly assigned to either a limited-use condition, in which they were asked to spend no more than 10 minutes per platform per day (about 30 minutes in total), or a usual-use condition, in which they continued to use social media as they normally would.
Participants in the limited-use condition were encouraged to use their phone's built-in settings to remind them when they reached their daily limit.
[od2] Loneliness was measured with the revised UCLA Loneliness Scale, a 20-item questionnaire in which participants rate statements such as "I lack companionship" on a 4-point scale, with higher scores indicating greater loneliness.
Depression was measured with the Beck Depression Inventory, a widely used 21-item measure of depressive symptoms.
Anxiety was measured with the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, and FOMO was measured with the 10-item Fear of Missing Out Scale.
All well-being measures were completed at baseline and again at the end of the three-week period.
Thirteen participants were excluded because they did not submit their usage screenshots on most days, leaving a final sample of 130 for the analyses.

== Results
Participants in the limited-use condition reduced their total social media use substantially, from an average of about 78 minutes per day at baseline to about 30 minutes per day, whereas use in the usual-use condition remained similar.
Loneliness decreased significantly more in the limited-use condition than in the usual-use condition, F(1, 128) = 6.42, p = .01.
Depression also decreased significantly more in the limited-use condition, F(1, 128) = 5.11, p = .03.
[mod] Further analysis showed that this reduction in depression was largest among participants who had reported higher levels of depression at baseline.
Both conditions showed significant decreases in anxiety and FOMO from baseline to the end of the study, but the two conditions did not differ from each other on these outcomes, both ps > .30.
There were no significant differences between conditions in social support, self-esteem or self-acceptance.

== Discussion
The results indicate that limiting social media use to around 30 minutes per day led to meaningful reductions in loneliness and depression over three weeks.
Because participants were randomly assigned and use was measured objectively, these findings provide stronger evidence than earlier correlational studies that social media use may contribute to poorer well-being.
[timp] Theoretically, the findings are consistent with social comparison theory, as reducing exposure to idealised images of others' lives may reduce unfavourable comparisons and the negative feelings they produce.
The finding that anxiety and FOMO decreased in both conditions suggests that simply monitoring one's own social media use each day may increase self-awareness and bring some benefits on its own.
The stronger effect for students who began the study with higher depression suggests that the intervention may be most helpful for those who are already struggling.
[imp] In practice, university counselling services could encourage students to set daily time limits on social media rather than asking them to give it up completely, which many students would find unrealistic.
[lim] One limitation is that the study only included iPhone users from a single private university, and the sample was mostly female, so the findings may not apply to other groups of students.
[lim~] Another limitation is that the study lasted only three weeks, so it is unclear whether the benefits would continue if students kept limiting their use.
[fut] Future research should follow participants for longer periods and should examine which specific features of social media, such as viewing others' photos or receiving comments, are most strongly linked to loneliness and depression.
Taken together, the findings suggest that using social media less, rather than not at all, may be a practical way for students to look after their mental health.
