== Abstract
Procrastination is common among university students, yet its consequences for well-being remain debated.
Some students claim that they work best under pressure and that putting off work reduces stress, whereas others believe that procrastination is harmful.
This study followed undergraduate students across a full semester to examine how procrastination was related to stress, physical illness and academic performance at different points in time.
Early in the semester, students who scored higher on procrastination reported less stress and fewer symptoms of illness than students who scored lower.
By the end of the semester, however, this pattern had reversed: procrastinators reported more stress and more symptoms, and they received lower grades on their assignments and examinations.
Across the whole semester, procrastination was associated with greater overall stress and illness.
These results suggest that procrastination is a self-defeating behaviour pattern that offers short-term relief but carries long-term costs.

== Introduction
[cd] Procrastination refers to the voluntary delay of an intended course of action despite expecting to be worse off as a result of the delay.
It is one of the most common problems reported by university students, with surveys suggesting that a large majority of undergraduates procrastinate at least some of the time and many consider it a serious problem.
Despite how common it is, students disagree about whether procrastination is actually harmful.
Some students argue that leaving work until the last minute helps them focus, because the pressure of a deadline increases their motivation and efficiency.
Others report that procrastination makes them anxious and leads to rushed, lower-quality work.
Previous research has produced mixed findings on this question.
Some studies have found that procrastinators obtain lower grades, while others have found little relationship between procrastination and performance once ability is taken into account.
[gap] Importantly, most earlier studies measured procrastination and its outcomes only once, so they could not show whether the costs and benefits of procrastination change over time.
[gap~] A single measurement taken early in a semester might show that procrastinators are relaxed, whereas a measurement taken just before deadlines might show the opposite.
[tf] The present research draws on the idea of self-defeating behaviour (Baumeister & Scher, 1988), which describes patterns in which people pursue an immediate benefit at the expense of a larger delayed cost.
[tf~] According to this view, people engage in self-defeating behaviours because the benefit, such as relief from an unpleasant task, is felt immediately, whereas the cost, such as poorer performance and stress, comes later and is easier to ignore.
If procrastination is a self-defeating behaviour, procrastinators should feel better than others early in the semester, when deadlines are far away, but worse than others later, when the delayed work must finally be completed.
[cd2] Stress, in this study, refers to the feeling that the demands placed on a person exceed their ability to cope.
[rq] The present study therefore asked how procrastination is related to stress, illness and academic performance at the beginning and end of a semester.
[h1] It was hypothesised that students higher in procrastination would report less stress and fewer illness symptoms than other students early in the semester.
[h2] It was further hypothesised that, by the end of the semester, students higher in procrastination would report more stress and illness symptoms than other students.
[h3] Finally, it was predicted that procrastination would be associated with poorer academic performance.

== Method
[method] This was a longitudinal correlational study in which the same students were measured at several points across one semester, without any manipulation by the researchers.
[sample] Participants were 44 undergraduate students enrolled in a health psychology course at a university in the United States, who took part as part of their course requirements.
[od] Procrastination was measured in the first week of the semester using the General Procrastination Scale, a 20-item questionnaire in which participants rated statements such as "I generally delay before starting on work I have to do" on a 5-point scale.
[od2] Stress and physical illness were measured with a symptom checklist asking how often participants had experienced symptoms such as headaches, colds, stomach problems and difficulty sleeping, along with a rating of how stressed they felt.
These measures were completed in the fourth week of the semester and again in the final week, shortly before examinations.
Participants also reported the number of visits they had made to health-care professionals during the semester.
Academic performance was recorded from the course instructor's records and included marks for a term paper and for two examinations.
The deadline for the term paper was announced at the start of the semester, and the date on which each paper was submitted was also recorded as an objective indicator of procrastination.
[ctrl] To check that any differences were not simply due to academic ability, participants' prior grade point averages were also obtained.

== Results
Scores on the procrastination scale were positively correlated with how late students handed in their term papers, r = .39, p = .01, supporting the validity of the scale.
Early in the semester, procrastination was negatively correlated with stress, r = −.33, p = .03, and with symptoms of illness, r = −.30, p = .05, meaning that procrastinators felt somewhat better at this point.
By the end of the semester, procrastination was positively correlated with stress, r = .29, p = .06, and with symptoms of illness, r = .31, p = .04.
Procrastinators also reported more visits to health-care professionals across the semester.
Procrastination was negatively correlated with term paper grades, r = −.42, p = .01, and with examination grades, r = −.37, p = .02.
These relationships with grades remained significant after controlling for students' prior grade point averages.

== Discussion
The findings show that the effects of procrastination depend on when they are measured.
Early in the semester, procrastinators reported less stress and fewer symptoms, which may explain why many students believe that putting off work is harmless.
By the end of the semester, however, procrastinators were more stressed and more often ill, and they performed worse on both their term papers and their examinations.
[timp] Theoretically, this pattern supports the view of procrastination as a self-defeating behaviour, in which short-term relief is gained at the cost of long-term well-being and performance.
The results also challenge the popular belief that procrastinators work better under pressure, since their work was graded lower even after their prior ability was taken into account.
[imp] In practical terms, universities could teach time-management strategies early in the semester and set intermediate deadlines for large assignments, so that students do not leave all of their work until the end.
[lim] A limitation of the study is its small sample of 44 students from a single course, which reduces statistical power and makes it difficult to generalise the findings.
[lim~] In addition, because the study was correlational, it cannot show that procrastination causes stress and illness; other factors, such as poor self-regulation, may influence both.
[fut] Future research could use larger and more diverse samples and could test experimentally whether interventions that reduce procrastination also reduce stress at the end of the semester.
In summary, putting off work may feel good at first, but the costs appear to catch up with students when it matters most.
