== Abstract
Over a billion people use Facebook, which offers an unprecedented opportunity to satisfy the basic human need for social connection.
Rather than enhancing well-being, however, the present findings suggest that Facebook use may undermine it.
Young adults were sent text messages five times per day for two weeks, asking how they felt, how worried and lonely they were, and how much they had used Facebook and interacted directly with other people since the previous message.
Multilevel analyses indicated that the more people used Facebook at one time point, the worse they felt at the next time point, and the more they used Facebook over the two weeks, the more their life satisfaction declined.
Direct interactions with other people did not predict declines in well-being.
There was no evidence that feeling bad led people to use Facebook more, and the effects were not explained by loneliness, gender, self-esteem or depression.
These results suggest that, on the surface, Facebook provides an invaluable resource for fulfilling the need for connection, but it may in fact have the opposite effect on young adults' well-being.

== Introduction
Facebook allows people to stay in contact with friends and family, share news and photographs and receive social support, all at any time of the day.
Because humans have a fundamental need to belong, a tool that makes social contact so easy might be expected to increase well-being.
Research on social relationships consistently shows that people with strong social ties are happier, healthier and live longer than people who are socially isolated.
Online communication might therefore be expected to bring the same benefits as face-to-face contact, particularly for people who find it difficult to meet others in person.
On the other hand, online interaction differs from face-to-face interaction in important ways, for example because it lacks facial expressions, tone of voice and physical touch.
[cd] Subjective well-being refers to how people evaluate their lives, and it is usually separated into two components.
[cd2] The first component, affective well-being, refers to how people feel from moment to moment, including the balance of their positive and negative emotions.
[cd3] The second component, cognitive well-being, refers to people's overall judgement of how satisfied they are with their lives.
Previous research on Facebook and well-being has produced mixed results.
Some studies have found that Facebook use is associated with greater social capital and higher self-esteem, particularly among people who feel insecure in face-to-face settings.
Other studies have found that heavier Facebook users report more loneliness, more envy and lower life satisfaction.
For instance, one survey of university students found that those who spent more time on Facebook were more likely to agree that other people had better lives than they did.
Another study found that passively browsing other people's profiles was associated with greater envy, whereas actively posting and messaging were not.
These mixed findings suggest that the relationship between Facebook use and well-being may depend on how, when and by whom Facebook is used.
[gap] A key problem is that almost all of this research has been cross-sectional, measuring Facebook use and well-being at a single point in time.
[gap~] Cross-sectional designs cannot show how Facebook use and well-being relate to each other over time, or whether Facebook use comes before changes in well-being or follows them.
In addition, most studies have asked people to estimate their Facebook use over long periods, which requires them to remember behaviour that is frequent, brief and easy to forget.
[method~] Experience sampling, in which people are repeatedly asked about their current experiences in daily life, offers a way to address both of these problems.
[tf] One theoretical reason to expect Facebook use to harm well-being comes from social comparison theory (Festinger, 1954), which proposes that people evaluate themselves by comparing their own lives and abilities with those of others.
[tf~] Because users tend to post flattering information, frequent exposure to others' highlights may lead to upward comparisons and feelings of envy and inadequacy.
At the same time, it is possible that the relationship works in the opposite direction, so that people who already feel bad turn to Facebook to cheer themselves up.
It is also possible that a third variable, such as loneliness or low self-esteem, leads people both to use Facebook more and to feel worse, which would make the two appear related even if one does not influence the other.
A design that measures both variables repeatedly over time allows researchers to test whether Facebook use comes before changes in well-being, while also taking such third variables into account.
[rq] The present study therefore examined how Facebook use influences the two components of subjective well-being over time in young adults.
[h1] It was hypothesised that greater Facebook use at one time point would predict worse affect at the next time point.
[h2] It was further hypothesised that greater Facebook use across the two-week period would predict declines in life satisfaction from the start to the end of the study.
[h3] In contrast, it was predicted that direct (face-to-face or telephone) interaction with other people would not predict declines in well-being.

== Method
[method] The study used an experience-sampling design, in which participants' Facebook use and well-being were measured repeatedly in daily life over two weeks without any manipulation by the researchers.
[sample] Participants were 82 young adults (53 women, 29 men; mean age = 19.5 years) from a university town in the Midwestern United States, all of whom had a Facebook account and a smartphone.
They were recruited through flyers and were paid for their participation.
At the start of the study, participants completed questionnaires measuring life satisfaction, depression, self-esteem, loneliness and the number of their Facebook friends.
Over the following 14 days, participants received five text messages per day at random times between 10 a.m. and midnight.
[od] Each text message linked to a short online survey in which participants rated how they felt "right now" on a slider scale from "very positive" to "very negative", which served as the measure of affect.
[od2] Each survey also asked participants how much they had used Facebook since the last survey, on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (a lot).
[od3] Direct social interaction was measured by asking how much participants had interacted with other people "directly" (face-to-face or on the phone) since the last survey, on the same 5-point scale.
Participants also rated how worried and how lonely they felt at each survey.
[od4] At the end of the 14 days, participants again completed the Satisfaction with Life Scale, a 5-item questionnaire in which they rate statements such as "I am satisfied with my life" on a 7-point scale.
Participants responded to an average of 83% of the text messages, indicating good compliance.
[ana] Because each participant provided many responses, the data were analysed using multilevel models, which take into account that responses from the same person are related to each other.

== Results
The more participants used Facebook between two text messages, the worse they felt at the second text message, B = −0.08, SE = 0.03, p = .003.
[rev] However, how participants felt at one text message did not predict how much they used Facebook before the next text message, B = 0.002, p = .90.
In contrast, the more participants interacted directly with other people, the better they felt over time, B = 0.08, p = .03.
The more participants used Facebook over the two weeks, the more their life satisfaction declined from the start to the end of the study, B = −0.41, p = .006.
Direct social interaction was not significantly related to changes in life satisfaction, B = 0.10, p = .57.
Loneliness was associated with greater Facebook use, B = 0.15, p < .001, but loneliness did not account for the relationship between Facebook use and declines in affect.
[mod] The relationship between Facebook use and affect did not differ according to participants' gender, self-esteem, depression or the size of their Facebook network.

== Discussion
These findings suggest that Facebook use predicts declines in both how people feel from moment to moment and how satisfied they are with their lives.
Importantly, there was no evidence that people used Facebook more when they felt bad, which makes it less likely that the results simply reflect unhappy people turning to Facebook.
The finding that direct interaction did not have the same negative effect suggests that it is not social contact in general that undermines well-being, but something specific about interacting through Facebook.
Several features of Facebook could explain this difference, including the tendency to present an idealised version of oneself, the large number of weak ties in most users' networks, and the ease of passively observing others without interacting.
The finding that loneliness predicted greater Facebook use, but did not explain its relationship with affect, suggests that lonely people may turn to Facebook for connection without receiving the benefits they are seeking.
[timp] Theoretically, these findings extend social comparison theory to everyday online behaviour, suggesting that frequent exposure to others' selectively positive posts may gradually reduce people's evaluations of their own lives.
[timp~] The results also challenge the assumption that tools which make social contact easier necessarily satisfy the human need for connection.
[imp] Practically, students and counsellors should be aware that frequent Facebook use may lower mood, and young people could be encouraged to balance online contact with direct interaction with friends and family.
[lim] One limitation is that, although the design tracked how variables changed over time, it was not experimental, so it cannot establish that Facebook use causes declines in well-being.
[lim~] Facebook use was also self-reported, and participants may not have estimated their use accurately even over short periods.
In addition, the sample was relatively small and consisted mainly of university students in one region of the United States.
[fut] Future research should experimentally manipulate Facebook use, for example by asking some participants to stop using it for a period, and should examine which specific activities on Facebook, such as passively scrolling versus posting, are responsible for these effects.
Taken together, the findings suggest that, rather than enhancing well-being, interacting with Facebook may predict the opposite outcome for young adults.
