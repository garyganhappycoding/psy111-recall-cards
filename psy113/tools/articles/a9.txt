== Abstract
Tests are usually seen as tools for measuring what students have learned, rather than as a way to improve learning.
In this experiment, students read short prose passages and then either restudied each passage or took a free-recall test on it, without receiving feedback.
Final recall was tested after 5 minutes, 2 days or 1 week.
After 5 minutes, restudying produced slightly better recall than testing, but after 2 days and 1 week, testing produced substantially better recall than restudying.
Students who restudied also predicted that they would remember more than students who were tested, even though the opposite was true in the long term.
These findings show that taking a test is a powerful learning event and that students may underestimate its benefits.

== Introduction
Most students prepare for examinations by rereading their notes and textbooks, often several times.
Surveys of study habits show that rereading is among the most popular study strategies, while practising recall without looking at one's notes is used much less often.
In education, tests are usually treated as neutral assessments that measure learning without changing it.
Students and teachers often view tests as stressful events that interrupt learning, and many students try to avoid practice tests until they feel fully prepared.
Teachers, in turn, may limit the number of quizzes they give because of the time needed to prepare and mark them.
If testing itself improves learning, however, these practices may be preventing students from using one of the most effective learning strategies available.
[cd] However, laboratory research has identified the testing effect, which refers to the finding that retrieving information from memory improves later retention of that information more than spending the same time restudying it.
[cd2] Retrieval practice, in turn, refers to any learning activity in which a learner deliberately recalls information from memory, such as answering questions or writing down everything they can remember.
Early demonstrations of the testing effect date back nearly a century, when schoolchildren who recited material from memory remembered it better than those who only reread it.
Since then, many laboratory studies have shown that people who are tested on word lists or word pairs remember them better on a later test than people who simply study the same items again.
Some researchers have argued that this benefit occurs only because testing gives learners extra exposure to the material, in the same way as restudying would.
Others have argued that the benefit occurs only when learners receive feedback, which tells them the correct answers they failed to retrieve.
Neither explanation can be tested unless the time spent on testing and restudying is equal and no feedback is given.
[gap] Despite this long history, most studies of the testing effect have used simple materials, such as lists of unrelated words, rather than the kind of connected prose that students read in their courses.
[gap~] In addition, many studies measured memory only shortly after learning, so it is not clear whether the benefits of testing last over the delays that typically separate studying from examinations.
Another unanswered question is whether students are aware of the benefits of testing, which may help to explain why they rarely use it.
[tf] One explanation for the testing effect is the retrieval effort account (Bjork, 1994), which proposes that the act of effortfully retrieving information strengthens memory and creates more routes for accessing it later.
[tf~] According to this account, restudying feels easier because the information is in front of the learner, but this ease does not produce the same lasting strengthening of memory as retrieving the information unaided.
This account also suggests that the benefit of testing may not appear immediately, because restudying gives learners extra exposure to the material just before the test, which may help them in the short term.
[rq] The present study asked whether taking a test on a prose passage, compared with restudying it, leads to better recall shortly after learning and after longer delays, and whether students can predict which strategy will help them more.
[h1] It was predicted that students who took a test would recall more of the passage than students who restudied it at all three retention intervals.
[h2] It was further predicted that the advantage of testing over restudying would increase as the delay between learning and the final test became longer.
[h3] Finally, it was expected that students who restudied would predict higher future recall than students who were tested, because rereading creates a feeling of familiarity.

== Method
[method] The study used an experiment with a mixed design, in which learning condition (test vs restudy) was manipulated within participants and retention interval (5 minutes, 2 days or 1 week) was manipulated between participants.
[sample] Participants were 120 undergraduate students at Washington University in St. Louis, who took part for course credit or payment.
[sample~] Participants were randomly assigned to one of the three retention-interval groups, with 40 students in each group.
Two prose passages from a reading-comprehension test were used, one about sea otters and one about the Sun, each about 275 words long and each divided into 30 idea units for scoring.
[manip] In the study phase, participants read each passage for 7 minutes, and then either reread the same passage for a further 7 minutes (restudy condition) or spent 7 minutes writing down as much of the passage as they could remember (test condition).
No feedback was given in the test condition, so participants in that condition were never shown the passage a second time.
[ctrl] Which passage was restudied and which was tested was counterbalanced across participants, so that any differences in passage difficulty could not explain the results.
After the study phase, participants solved simple maths problems for two minutes to prevent them from rehearsing the passages.
At the end of the study phase, participants rated how much of each passage they expected to remember in one week, on a scale from 1 to 7.
[od] Final recall was measured by asking participants to write down everything they could remember from each passage, and recall was scored as the percentage of the 30 idea units that each participant correctly reproduced.
Two independent raters scored a sample of the recall protocols, and their scores agreed closely, indicating that the scoring was reliable.

== Results
After 5 minutes, participants recalled slightly more of the restudied passage (81%) than of the tested passage (75%), t(39) = 2.12, p = .04.
After 2 days, participants recalled more of the tested passage (68%) than of the restudied passage (54%), t(39) = 4.20, p < .001.
After 1 week, participants again recalled more of the tested passage (56%) than of the restudied passage (42%), t(39) = 4.59, p < .001.
[int] A significant interaction between learning condition and retention interval, F(2, 117) = 18.9, p < .001, showed that the effect of testing depended on the delay before the final test.
Forgetting was much faster after restudying than after testing: from 5 minutes to 1 week, recall of the restudied passage dropped by 39 percentage points, whereas recall of the tested passage dropped by 19 percentage points.
[res3] Participants predicted that they would remember more of the restudied passage (M = 4.8) than of the tested passage (M = 4.0), p < .01.

== Discussion
These results show that taking a single recall test on a prose passage produced better long-term retention than restudying it, even though no feedback was provided.
The short-term advantage of restudying, followed by a long-term advantage of testing, shows that the benefits of a learning strategy cannot be judged from performance immediately after learning.
This pattern is important because students often judge the success of their studying by how well they feel they know the material at the end of a study session.
Because restudying produces a strong feeling of familiarity, students may conclude that it is working, even though the material will be forgotten more quickly.
The results also show that the benefits of testing did not depend on feedback, since participants in the test condition never saw the passage again after the first reading.
[timp] Theoretically, the results support the retrieval effort account, as the effortful act of retrieval appeared to slow forgetting, while the extra exposure provided by restudying produced only a brief benefit.
The finding that students expected restudying to be more effective suggests that learners may confuse the feeling that material is familiar with actually having learned it.
[imp] In practice, teachers could replace some review sessions with low-stakes quizzes, and students could test themselves, for example by using flashcards or writing summaries from memory, instead of simply rereading their notes.
[lim] One limitation is that the passages were short and the final test used the same format as the practice test, so it remains unclear whether the benefits would transfer to longer course materials or to different kinds of examination questions.
[lim~] In addition, the participants were students at a selective American university, who may differ from other learners in their reading and study skills.
[fut] Future studies could examine the testing effect with real course content in classroom settings and could test whether providing feedback after practice tests increases the benefits further.
In conclusion, tests are not only a way of measuring learning but also a powerful way of producing it.
