== Abstract
Many people find it easier to remember something when they return to the place where they first learned it.
This study tested whether memory is better when information is recalled in the same environment in which it was learned.
Divers learned lists of words either on a beach or underwater and then recalled them either in the same environment or in the other one.
Words were recalled better when the learning and recall environments matched than when they did not.
These results provide strong evidence for context-dependent memory in natural environments.

== Introduction
Everyday experience suggests that the place where we learn something can help us remember it.
For example, people sometimes forget why they walked into a room, but remember again when they go back to where they started.
[cd] Context-dependent memory refers to the finding that information is easier to recall when the physical environment at recall matches the environment in which it was learned.
[tf] This idea is explained by the encoding specificity principle (Tulving & Thomson, 1973), which states that memory is best when the cues present at retrieval match the cues present when the information was first encoded.
[tf~] According to this principle, features of the environment, such as sights and sounds, become stored together with what is learned and can later act as retrieval cues.
Earlier laboratory studies found some evidence for context effects, for instance when the colour or smell of a room was changed between learning and testing.
Other studies have found that people's internal state, such as their mood, can also act as a context, so that information learned while happy is easier to recall when happy again.
These findings suggest that memories are stored together with information about the situation in which they were formed.
[gap] However, most of these studies used small changes in context within a laboratory, and their results were often weak or inconsistent.
[gap~] Very few studies had tested context-dependent memory in two very different natural environments.
Divers offer a useful opportunity to do this, because being underwater is extremely different from being on land, in terms of sight, sound, temperature and breathing.
[rq] The present study asked whether divers would recall words better when they were tested in the same environment in which they had learned them.
[hyp] It was hypothesised that recall would be higher when the learning and recall environments were the same than when they were different.

== Method
[method] The study was an experiment in which every participant took part in all four combinations of learning environment and recall environment.
[sample] Participants were 18 members of a university diving club, who took part during a diving trip in Scotland.
[manip] Each participant learned four lists of 36 unrelated words, each list in one of four conditions: learn on land and recall on land, learn on land and recall underwater, learn underwater and recall underwater, and learn underwater and recall on land.
Words were presented through a tape recorder, and when on land, divers sat at the edge of the water wearing their diving equipment, so that the only difference between conditions was the environment.
[ctrl] The order of the conditions was varied across participants, so that tiredness or practice could not explain the results.
[od] Recall was measured by asking participants to write down as many words as they could remember from each list, and the score was the number of words correctly recalled.

== Results
When words were learned and recalled in the same environment, participants recalled an average of about 13 words.
When words were learned in one environment and recalled in the other, participants recalled an average of about 8.5 words.
This difference was statistically significant, p < .05, and represents roughly 40% better recall when the environments matched.
The effect was found both for words learned on land and for words learned underwater.

== Discussion
The results show that recall was clearly better when divers were tested in the same environment where they had learned the words.
The size of this effect is notable, as a 40% difference is much larger than the effects found in earlier laboratory studies of context.
This may be because being underwater is so different from being on land that it provides many distinctive cues that can later help or hinder recall.
[timp] These findings support the encoding specificity principle, suggesting that environmental cues are stored with memories and help people retrieve them.
[imp] In practice, divers who are trained on land may need to practise important procedures underwater, and students may benefit from studying in conditions similar to those of the exam.
[lim] One limitation is that the sample was small, with only 18 divers, all of whom were experienced members of one diving club.
[fut] Future research could examine whether simply imagining the learning environment can produce the same memory benefits as being physically present.
These results show that where we learn something can have a large influence on what we later remember.
