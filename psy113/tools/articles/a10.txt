== Abstract
Urbanisation has many benefits, but it is also associated with higher rates of mental illness, including depression.
At the same time, people in cities spend less time in natural environments, and it has been suggested that this loss of contact with nature may contribute to poorer mental health.
This study tested whether a short walk in nature, compared with a walk in an urban setting, would reduce rumination and activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex, a brain region linked to negative self-focused thinking.
Healthy adults living in a city were randomly assigned to a 90-minute walk in either a natural or an urban environment, and self-reported rumination and brain activity were measured before and after the walk.
Participants who walked in nature showed decreases in self-reported rumination and in subgenual prefrontal cortex activity, whereas participants who walked in the urban setting did not.
Heart rate and breathing rate did not differ between the two walks, suggesting that the effects were not simply due to differences in physical exertion.
These findings suggest a possible mechanism through which contact with nature may support mental health in an increasingly urbanised world.

== Introduction
More than half of the world's population now lives in cities, and this proportion is expected to grow to nearly 70% by 2050.
City living offers access to jobs, education and health care, but it has also been associated with a higher risk of mental illness, including depression and anxiety disorders.
One possible contributor to this risk is that city dwellers have fewer opportunities to spend time in natural environments, such as parks, forests and green spaces.
Several studies have found that people who live near green space report better mental health, and that spending time in nature is associated with improved mood and attention.
For example, large population studies have found that residents of greener neighbourhoods report lower levels of psychological distress, even after taking income and education into account.
Experimental studies have also shown that viewing pictures of nature, compared with pictures of urban scenes, can improve performance on attention tasks.
However, many of these studies were correlational, and people who choose to live near green space may differ in other ways from those who do not, for example in their health or lifestyle.
[cd] Rumination refers to a repetitive pattern of thought focused on negative aspects of the self, such as one's failures, mistakes and shortcomings.
Rumination is a well-established risk factor for depression, as people who ruminate frequently are more likely to develop depressive episodes and to remain depressed for longer.
[cd2] Neuroimaging research has linked rumination to activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex (sgPFC), a region at the front of the brain that is particularly active during sadness and negative self-focused thinking.
Activity in this region is elevated in people with depression and is reduced by effective treatments.
Because the sgPFC appears to be closely linked to rumination, measuring its activity provides a way to examine whether an experience changes the neural processes underlying negative self-focused thinking, rather than relying on self-reports alone.
Self-reports can be influenced by participants' expectations, so combining them with brain measures provides stronger evidence than either measure alone.
[gap] Although research has shown that nature experience is associated with better mood, little is known about the psychological and neural mechanisms through which nature might benefit mental health.
[gap~] In particular, no study has examined whether nature experience reduces rumination or the brain activity associated with it.
[tf] One theoretical explanation for the benefits of nature comes from attention restoration theory (Kaplan, 1995), which proposes that natural environments capture attention gently and effortlessly, allowing the mental resources used for focused attention to recover.
[tf~] By drawing attention outwards towards interesting but undemanding features of the environment, nature may also reduce the tendency to focus inwards on negative thoughts about oneself.
Urban environments, in contrast, are full of stimuli that demand attention, such as traffic and crowds, and may therefore offer fewer opportunities for this kind of restoration.
[rq] The present study investigated whether a brief walk in nature, compared with a walk in an urban environment, would reduce rumination and sgPFC activity in healthy city dwellers.
[h1] It was hypothesised that participants who walked in nature would show a greater decrease in self-reported rumination than participants who walked in an urban setting.
[h2] It was further hypothesised that participants who walked in nature would show a greater decrease in sgPFC activity than participants who walked in an urban setting.
[h3] Because the two walks were matched in length and difficulty, it was predicted that physiological measures of exertion, such as heart rate and breathing rate, would not differ between the conditions.

== Method
[method] The study used a randomised experiment in which participants were assigned to one of two walking conditions, with measures taken before and after the walk.
[sample] Participants were 38 healthy adults (19 women, 19 men; mean age = 26.6 years) who lived in an urban area of California and had no history of mental illness.
[ctrl] Participants with a history of psychiatric disorders were excluded, because the study aimed to examine effects in people without existing mental health conditions.
[manip] Participants were randomly assigned to take a 90-minute walk either in a natural area near a university campus, consisting of grassland with scattered oak trees and shrubs, or along a busy four-lane road in a nearby city.
Both walks were of similar length and difficulty, and participants walked alone while following a map and were asked not to speak to others or use their phones.
[od] Rumination was measured before and after the walk with the brooding subscale of the Ruminative Response Scale, which asks participants how often they think about statements such as "Why do I always react this way?" on a scale from 1 (almost never) to 4 (almost always).
[od2] Activity in the sgPFC was measured using arterial spin labelling, a brain-imaging technique that estimates blood flow to specific brain regions, with participants scanned before and after the walk.
[od3] Heart rate and breathing rate were recorded throughout the walk using a portable monitor worn on the chest.
Participants also completed a measure of positive and negative emotion before and after the walk.

== Results
Before the walk, the two groups did not differ in rumination, sgPFC activity or emotion, indicating that random assignment had produced comparable groups.
Participants in the nature condition showed a significant decrease in rumination from before to after the walk, t(18) = 2.92, p = .009, whereas participants in the urban condition showed no significant change, t(18) = 0.21, p = .84.
The interaction between condition and time was significant, F(1, 36) = 4.99, p = .03, showing that the change in rumination differed between the two conditions.
Participants in the nature condition also showed a significant decrease in blood flow to the sgPFC, whereas participants in the urban condition did not, with a significant condition × time interaction, p = .02.
Heart rate and breathing rate did not differ significantly between the two conditions, all ps > .30.
Positive emotion did not change significantly in either condition, and negative emotion decreased slightly in both conditions, with no significant difference between them.

== Discussion
These results show that a 90-minute walk in nature reduced both self-reported rumination and activity in a brain region associated with negative self-focused thinking.
Because the two walks did not differ in heart rate or breathing, the effects are unlikely to be explained by differences in physical exercise.
The absence of differences in general positive and negative emotion suggests that nature may specifically affect self-focused negative thinking, rather than mood in general.
This specificity is important because it suggests that the benefits of nature are not simply a matter of feeling more relaxed or happier after a pleasant walk.
Instead, nature experience may change a particular pattern of thinking that is known to increase the risk of depression.
These findings are consistent with earlier studies showing that time in nature improves attention, since reduced rumination may free attention for other tasks.
[timp] Theoretically, these findings provide evidence for a possible mechanism linking nature experience to mental health, suggesting that attention restoration may work in part by reducing rumination.
[timp~] The findings also extend previous research by showing that the benefits of nature can be observed not only in self-reports but also in brain activity.
[imp] In practice, urban planners could protect and expand green spaces within cities, and mental health professionals could recommend regular walks in nature as an accessible way to reduce rumination.
[lim] A limitation of this study is its small sample of 38 healthy adults from one region of California, which limits how far the findings can be generalised, especially to people with depression.
[lim~] In addition, the study measured only the immediate effects of a single walk, so it remains unclear how long the benefits last or whether repeated exposure would produce larger effects.
[fut] Future research should examine whether nature experience reduces rumination in people with depression and whether regular access to green space has lasting effects on mental health.
As cities continue to grow, understanding how contact with nature benefits the mind may become increasingly important for public health.
